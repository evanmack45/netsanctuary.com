How it works

  1. Make a short list of companies that hold your information.
  2. Find each company's own page for Texas privacy requests.
  3. Write the letter in the builder.
  4. Send it yourself, from your own email or the company's form.
  5. Write the date in the tracker. If the company says no or stays silent, appeal.

What the Texas laws say

  • Texas has a data broker law. A broker that meets its size tests has to register with the Texas Secretary of State and pay a $300 fee. The Secretary of State publishes a searchable list. Sources: Bus. & Com. Code ch. 510, sections 510.003, 510.005, 510.006; Texas Secretary of State.
  • The broker has to give the state a link to a page that explains how to use your rights under the Texas privacy law. A broker with a website also has to post a notice that says it is a data broker. Source: ch. 510, sections 510.004 and 510.005(b)(2-a).
  • The Texas Data Privacy and Security Act lets Texas residents ask a company to delete their personal data, stop selling it, or show it to them. It took effect July 1, 2024. Source: Bus. & Com. Code ch. 541, sections 541.001(7) and 541.051.
  • The company has up to 45 days to answer. It can extend once by 45 more days if it tells you why within the first 45. If it says no, it has to tell you why and how to appeal. It has to answer your appeal in writing within 60 days. Source: ch. 541, sections 541.052(b), 541.052(c), 541.053(c).
  • If the company denies your appeal, it has to tell you how to complain to the Texas Attorney General. The Attorney General has a privacy complaint form, and only the Attorney General can enforce the Act. Sources: ch. 541, sections 541.053(d), 541.151, 541.152; Texas Attorney General privacy page.
  • The Secretary of State keeps the list. It says it has no authority to regulate a broker's business practices, investigate it, or enforce these rules. Source: Texas Secretary of State.

A note on numbering. Some guides still cite chapter 509 for the data broker law. The Legislature moved it to chapter 510 on September 1, 2025. Chapter 509 is now a different law, about minors online. The statute pages above show the current numbers.

What can go wrong

  • A company may refuse. Both Texas laws leave out "publicly available information," which includes government records. Spokeo's own opt-out page says it uses only publicly available information and that such data is exempt from state privacy laws. It offers an opt-out anyway. If a letter fails, a company's own opt-out page may work. Sources: ch. 541, sections 541.001(19) and (27); ch. 510, section 510.002(b)(3); Spokeo opt-out page.
  • Small companies are left out. The Privacy Act applies to a company that is not a small business by the U.S. Small Business Administration's definition. Source: ch. 541, section 541.002(a)(3).
  • A company may ask you to prove who you are. The law lets it hold off until it can confirm the request. What you send is your decision. This pack never asks for it, and we never see it. Source: ch. 541, sections 541.001(2) and 541.052(e).
  • Deletion can mean suppression. For data a company got from a source other than you, the law treats it as compliant with a deletion request if it keeps a record of the request and the minimum data needed to keep you deleted, or opts you out of other uses. That is not the same as erasing every copy. Source: ch. 541, section 541.052(f).
  • Your data can come back. Spokeo says "your information may reappear on Spokeo in the future without notice." Source: Spokeo opt-out page.
  • A free Texas form already exists. DROP Texas (droptexas.com) is a free form, run by a private company, that sends opt-out-of-sale requests to the companies it names. If you only want that, it is less work than this pack. The comparison page says when this pack is the better fit and when it is not. Source: DROP Texas help page.
  • The letters are for Texans. The Act covers residents of Texas. California residents have a separate state tool, DROP. Sources: ch. 541, section 541.001(7); California Privacy Protection Agency, DROP.

Why we do not send it for you

Sending requests for someone makes us their agent. That means holding their name and home addresses at the least. We do not want that data, and a leak of it would hurt you.

So you send every request yourself, from your own email or the company's own form. The Act's agent rule covers opt-outs of targeted ads and data sales. It does not mention deletion requests. Source: ch. 541, section 541.055(e).
